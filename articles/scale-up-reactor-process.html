From Lab to Production — Scaling Up Your Reactor Process

Scale-Up · 8 min read
WEIHAI ZHENGWEI MACHINERY CO.,LTD · Chemical Reactor Specialists

Scaling up a chemical process from lab to production is one of the most misunderstood aspects of reactor engineering. The mistake is assuming that “bigger is just bigger” — that if a reaction works in a 5L lab reactor, it will work in a 5,000L production reactor with proportional dimensions.

It will not. Mixing, heat transfer, and mass transfer all behave differently at different scales. Ignoring these differences is how batches get ruined, how reactors get redesigned, and how projects get delayed by months.

This guide explains what changes during scale-up and how to plan for it.

1. Geometric Similarity — The Foundation

The first principle of scale-up is geometric similarity: the ratio of every dimension in the production reactor should match the ratio in the lab reactor.

This means:

  • Diameter-to-height ratio stays the same
  • Impeller diameter-to-tank diameter ratio stays the same
  • Impeller clearance-from-bottom ratio stays the same
  • Baffle width-to-tank diameter ratio stays the same

Without geometric similarity, you are not scaling up — you are building something entirely different. The flow patterns, mixing characteristics, and heat transfer performance will all change in unpredictable ways.

2. Mixing Time Increases With Scale

Mixing time does not scale linearly. In a 5L reactor, complete mixing might take 30 seconds. In a 5,000L reactor at geometric similarity, mixing time might be 5-10 minutes.

This matters for:

  • Reagent addition: If you add reagent faster than it mixes, local concentrations can cause side reactions
  • Temperature control: Poor mixing creates hot spots where reactions proceed too fast
  • Product quality: Inconsistent mixing leads to batch-to-batch variation

3. Heat Transfer Coefficient Drops at Scale

The heat transfer coefficient (U) does not stay constant during scale-up. As reactor volume increases, the surface-area-to-volume ratio decreases, meaning less heat transfer area per unit of reaction mass.

This is why large reactors often require internal coils or external heat exchanger loops — the jacket alone cannot remove heat fast enough at production scale.

4. Mass Transfer Limitations in Large Reactors

For gas-liquid reactions (hydrogenation, oxidation, fermentation), mass transfer becomes a limiting factor at scale. The gas-liquid interfacial area per unit volume decreases as reactor size increases, and gas distribution becomes less uniform.

This is why hydrogenation reactors at production scale often use specialized impellers (Rushton turbines, gas-inducing impellers) designed to maximize gas-liquid contact.

5. When to Use a Pilot Reactor

A pilot reactor (typically 100-1,000L) is recommended when:

  • The reaction is highly exothermic (heat removal is critical)
  • The process involves gas-liquid mass transfer (hydrogenation, oxidation)
  • Product quality is sensitive to mixing time or temperature control
  • The reaction has never been run at production scale before
  • Regulatory requirements demand process validation data
The pilot reactor is not a luxury. It is a risk mitigation tool. The cost of a pilot reactor is always less than the cost of a failed production batch or a reactor redesign after commissioning.
