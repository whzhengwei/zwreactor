Material Selection Guide for Reactors

Material Selection · 6 min read
WEIHAI ZHENGWEI MACHINERY CO.,LTD · Chemical Reactor Specialists

Material selection is the first and most consequential decision in reactor design. Get it wrong and you are looking at premature corrosion, unexpected downtime, or worse — a safety incident. Get it right and your reactor runs for decades with minimal maintenance.

The problem is that material choice depends on more than just “what is corrosive.” Temperature, chloride concentration, pH, flow velocity, and even weld procedure all affect which alloy will actually survive your service life. This guide walks through the most common reactor materials and when to use each one.

1. 304 Stainless Steel — The Workhorse (With Limits)

304 is the most common reactor material, and for good reason: it handles a wide range of mild chemicals at moderate temperatures, it is readily available, and it costs about half of 316L.

But 304 has one critical weakness: chloride stress corrosion cracking (SCC). If your medium contains chlorides and your operating temperature exceeds about 60°C, 304 becomes a liability. Even trace amounts of chloride — things like residual cleaning agents or contaminated feedstocks — can initiate cracking at weld joints.

Rule of thumb: If your medium has any chloride content and you operate above 60°C, do not use 304. Use 316L at minimum, or consider duplex if conditions are severe.

2. 316L Stainless Steel — The Standard Choice

316L adds molybdenum (2-3%) to the 304 formulation, which significantly improves resistance to pitting and crevice corrosion — especially in chloride environments.

316L is the default material for most chemical reactors. It handles temperatures up to about 300°C, resists a broad range of organic and inorganic acids, and is acceptable for most food and pharmaceutical applications.

The “L” designation means low carbon (≤0.03%), which reduces the risk of sensitization — chromium carbide precipitation at grain boundaries during welding that leaves adjacent zones vulnerable to corrosion.

When 316L is sufficient: Non-chloride or low-chloride media, temperatures below 150°C, batch operations with regular cleaning, and most pharmaceutical applications.

3. Duplex 2205 — High Strength, High Corrosion Resistance

Duplex 2205 combines austenitic and ferritic microstructures, giving it roughly twice the yield strength of 316L and significantly better resistance to chloride stress corrosion cracking.

This makes duplex ideal for:

  • High-chloride environments (seawater, bleach, chlorinated organics)
  • High-temperature chloride service (>60°C with chlorides)
  • Applications where wall thickness can be reduced (higher strength = thinner walls)
  • Sulfide-containing environments (oil & gas)

Duplex costs about 1.5-2x 316L per kilogram, but the higher strength means you may need less material overall. For many chloride services, duplex is the most cost-effective choice when you factor in total lifecycle cost.

4. Hastelloy and Titanium — When Stainless Is Not Enough

For extreme corrosion environments — concentrated acids, hot chlorides, oxidizing media — nickel-based alloys and titanium become necessary. Hastelloy C276 handles wet chlorine, hypochlorite, and concentrated chloride salts. Titanium excels in oxidizing acids and seawater service.

These materials are expensive — often 5-10x the cost of 316L per kilogram. But for the right application, they deliver service lives measured in decades rather than months.

5. The Cost Trap: Cheaper Material, More Expensive Outcome

Here is what I see too often: a client specifies 304 because it is the cheapest option on paper. Three months later, the reactor is leaking at the welds. The replacement cost — including downtime, emergency shipping, and production losses — is 10-20x the original material price difference.

The real question is not “what does the material cost?” It is “what does the wrong material cost?”
